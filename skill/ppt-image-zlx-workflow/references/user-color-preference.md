# User Color Preference

Use this file when deciding project color direction. Treat it as a taste bias, not a fixed palette.

## Core Taste

The user prefers mature, polished, presentation-grade visuals with clear hierarchy, restrained color distribution, and material depth. The preferred look is not "more blue"; it is controlled atmosphere, meaningful contrast, and a memorable visual anchor.

Avoid making future decks overly blue just because many reference slides are blue. Blue is acceptable, often useful, and sometimes the strongest choice when the topic, brand, technology context, product material, or evidence environment supports it. The risk is automatic blue, not blue itself.

Anti-AI correction must not become overcorrection. Do not remove atmosphere, glow, gradients, depth, futuristic cues, or strong visual drama merely because they are common in AI images. Keep them when they have a clear semantic role, disciplined proportion, and good craft.

Confirmed user correction: blue, cyan, and blue-purple are not banned. They are useful when they act as screen glow, video signal, system connection, night-event depth, brand/industry atmosphere, or another named visual role. The failure mode is overuse, same-strength glow everywhere, or treating blue-purple as the default technology answer. The opposite failure mode is overcorrection: stripping cold light from topics that naturally need screens, video, signal processing, or digital atmosphere.

Material hierarchy is a preferred way to solve color sameness. Before changing palette, separate surfaces such as paper/report panels, glass monitors, matte metal devices, translucent overlays, signal lines, evidence photos, and nameplates. Let brightness, opacity, reflection, shadow, texture, and edge weight create hierarchy so accent colors do not have to carry every distinction.

## Dark Theme Signals

- Backgrounds are usually deep navy, blue-black, charcoal, or near-black, but they work because of depth and lighting, not because of hue alone.
- Strong pages use local light sources, rim light, glow, lens-like depth, or realistic object lighting around a main visual anchor.
- Accents are concentrated. Cyan, electric blue, violet, orange, gold, and green can all work when used as controlled highlights.
- The best dark slides avoid flat same-color cards. They use spatial layers, object depth, real/semireal scenes, dashboard material, or meaningful diagrams.
- Orange/gold examples show that warm accents are welcome when they come from brand, achievement, business value, geography, or energy rather than random decoration.

## Dark AI Technology Direction

Use this direction as one valid option for AI, technology, data platform, digital ecosystem, intelligent system, cloud center, future industry, and similar dark-theme projects.

This direction is not a generic blue template. It works when blue acts as spatial order, cold light, data flow, intelligence, system depth, or product-screen illumination.

- Base: deep navy, blue-black, graphite, or near-black with visible atmospheric depth instead of flat black.
- Main visual anchor: AI core, energy ring, planet, digital city, system center, product device, data hub, or another topic-specific object. Use only one dominant anchor.
- Light logic: keep glow, gradients, lens depth, rings, and futuristic atmosphere when they explain intelligence, scale, data motion, or system connection.
- Color behavior: dominant dark base, controlled cyan/electric-blue highlight, optional violet or ice-white high point, and optional warm accent for value, achievement, growth, or business outcome.
- Information behavior: use white or low-blue text for normal reading, bright blue only for key numbers or system signals, and avoid making every label, icon, line, and border glow.
- Composition patterns: center-radiating AI core, left-information/right-world visual, data metrics around a single symbolic object, or narrative scene plus restrained KPI group.
- Risk control: if the page still makes sense after replacing the topic with any other AI topic, the visual is too generic. Strengthen the topic-specific object, scene, or evidence role.

Approximate distribution can start around `72 / 16 / 8 / 4`: dark atmosphere / blue-cyan system light / white readability / optional violet or warm signal. Adapt this to the topic instead of copying the reference slides.

## Light Theme Signals

- Light pages are not pure white templates. They use pale blue-gray, misty white, soft gradients, subtle texture, and low-contrast depth.
- Blue often acts as an order color, but warm gold, orange, red ribbon, and soft natural colors can carry ceremony, achievement, growth, or narrative energy.
- Good light pages preserve whitespace, use fine lines and soft shadows, and avoid heavy blocks unless the page needs strong comparison.
- Background atmosphere can be mountains, clouds, institutional paper, product environment, or abstract material depth when it supports the topic.

## Preferred Color Behavior

- Default external palette source: call WebGradients first for project atmosphere and material color direction, especially for dark, cinematic, product, technology, video, sports, broadcast, event, and high-end commercial presentation pages. Use other libraries such as Ant Design Colors, Tailwind Colors, Material, or ColorBrewer when the page needs stricter UI/data semantics, neutral report structure, categorical data, or brand-system alignment.
- Use WebGradients as an atmosphere and material-gradient source, not as a decorative gradient background library. Prefer mature dark, cool, metallic, misty, or restrained warm gradients that support the topic. Avoid candy gradients, pastel lifestyle gradients, full-page saturated gradient washes, and visually attractive gradients that do not explain the content.
- Use one dominant background family, one main accent, one optional contrast accent, and very limited signal colors.
- Keep approximate distribution disciplined, often around `70 / 20 / 8 / 2`.
- When using an external color library such as Ant Design Colors, Tailwind Colors, Material, or ColorBrewer, use it as a tonal source and role system, not as a UI-template source. Borrow color ramps, contrast discipline, and semantic color logic; do not borrow dashboard widgets, button-like section headers, heavy admin cards, or product-interface layout language unless the page is intentionally a product UI.
- Prefer tonal depth inside one selected color family over a flat one-accent page. A successful same-family direction should use multiple steps for spatial background, raised surfaces, borders, icons, headings, signal lines, glow, and high-emphasis objects. Do not collapse a deck into neutral dark plus one repeated blue, green, or gold.
- Preserve information modules while improving their shells. If a page needs market judgment, evidence, capability, demand, or comparison modules, keep those modules visible and aligned; make the containers more mature through shared parent surfaces, material depth, thin dividers, hierarchy, and tonal steps instead of deleting modules to reduce UI flavor.
- Do not make cyan + orange the habitual answer. The user does not want repeated decks to converge on that pairing. Treat cyan + orange as one possible project-specific solution, not a personal default.
- When cyan + orange is used, define why it belongs to the current topic: for example cyan for system depth, data flow, product illumination, or technical order; orange only for value, alert, growth, energy, outcome, or a small decision signal. If those roles cannot be named, choose another direction.
- Prefer palette variety across projects when the topic allows it. Consider alternatives such as graphite + muted green, charcoal + amber, mist white + restrained red-gold, deep neutral + violet, paper white + graphite + one brand accent, or other topic-led combinations before returning to cyan + orange.
- Let material contrast do some hierarchy work: panel translucency, fine borders, soft shadows, depth, blur, reflection, and local highlights.
- Change accent emphasis by page role. Cover and closing pages can be more atmospheric; evidence and architecture pages should be clearer and more restrained; process pages should keep functional colors quiet.
- Choose colors from topic meaning, real images, brand cues, and page role before applying this preference.
- Do not force non-blue palettes for their own sake. If blue is the best semantic or brand fit, use blue deliberately and prevent sameness through material contrast, secondary accents, scene logic, and page-role variation.

## Non-Blue Directions To Consider

- Charcoal + warm amber/gold for achievement, finance, strategy, energy, manufacturing, and executive summaries.
- Deep graphite + teal/green for sustainability, operations, healthcare, quality, and system health.
- Ink black + magenta/violet only when the topic is entertainment, innovation, or high-energy digital experience.
- Warm white + gold/red-orange for awards, annual review, culture, milestones, and human narratives.
- Mist white + muted green/teal for ecology, medicine, education, service, and public-sector topics.
- Paper white + graphite + restrained brand accent for policy, research, consulting, and formal reporting.

## Avoid

- Default AI blue-purple neon as the automatic visual answer.
- Reusing cyan + orange as the automatic technology/business palette across unrelated decks.
- Cyan icons, cyan headings, cyan borders, cyan glow, and orange highlights applied with the same hierarchy on every page.
- Rejecting blue only to prove variety.
- Full-page saturated gradients with no semantic reason.
- Same-color card grids where every module has equal visual weight.
- Overcorrecting color discipline into a flat, thin, one- or two-color page with weak material depth.
- Removing needed modules just to avoid a dashboard feeling. The better fix is to keep module clarity and improve hierarchy, shell design, material, and tonal steps.
- Directly importing Ant Design, Tailwind, Material, or similar UI component language into a presentation page: blue filled section headers, button-like tabs, admin dashboard cards, status chips everywhere, or default product-interface grouping.
- Generic glassmorphism, glow borders, decorative particles, or grid backgrounds without topic meaning.
- Flattening the page into conservative, low-energy blocks after trying to remove AI flavor.
- Copying the reference screenshots' exact palettes or layouts.
- Overcorrecting away from blue so aggressively that the deck ignores real topic or brand cues.

## Decision Rule

For every new deck, consider at least two plausible color directions before choosing. Name why the selected direction fits the topic and why the rejected directions are weaker. If the best direction is blue, make it specific: define what kind of blue, why it belongs, what secondary color prevents sameness, and which AI-looking blue treatments are forbidden. If the best direction is not blue, explain why the topic benefits from that departure rather than treating non-blue as automatically superior.
